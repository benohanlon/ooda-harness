## Literary bearings

- [Homer, Iliad 2, passage beginning at 631](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad2.html) identifies Odysseus's fleet of twelve ships with vermilion prows. [The Greek lexicon entry](https://logeion.uchicago.edu/%CE%BC%CE%B9%CE%BB%CF%84%CE%BF%CF%80%CE%AC%CF%81%CE%B7%CE%BF%CF%82) explains the red-cheeked epithet through red-painted bows. This supports a visible red prow, rather than requiring a red sail. It does not establish a special mechanical capability.
- [Homer, Odyssey 8, passage beginning at 555](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerOdyssey8.html) describes the Phaeacian ships as understanding people's thoughts and travelling without ordinary pilots or steering oars. These are the hosts' ships, distinct from Odysseus's original fleet. That is a particularly useful literary analogy for a harness that carries human intent, provided the distinction remains explicit.
- Treat wine-dark sea as the chosen poetic visual bearing. Deep purple, black-blue and muted oxblood can convey it. This palette is an artistic interpretation, not a claim that one English colour resolves Homer's language.

## Proposed visual direction

Odysseus remains the engineer's archetype. The Phaeacian vessel is the proposed image of the harness itself, carrying the sovereign's intent towards their chosen destination. This is our application of the literary passage. It does not make Odysseus the builder of the Phaeacians' ships or transfer their properties to his original fleet.

An original horizontal illustration can use the supplied woodcut reference's rhythmic waves and strong ship silhouette, a visibly red-painted bow and a wine-dark sea. Keep the crew and rigging legible enough to suggest a working vessel. A distant bearing or opening in the weather gives the journey direction. This is a proposed composition, not a generated or accepted cover.
